
### Theorem 3.1
Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be bouned open set with Lipschitz boundary and $u_0 \in W^{1,2}(\Omega)$. The problem
$$
(D)\qquad \inf \left\{ I(u) = \frac{1}{2} \int_\Omega  |\nabla u(x)|^2 \, dx : u \in u_0+W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)\right\} = m
$$
has one and only one solution $\overline{u} \in u_0 + W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$.

> [!tip] Recall: What do these assumptions mean?
>
> **$\Omega$ bounded:** The domain has finite size, so integrals over $\Omega$ are finite.
>
> **Lipschitz boundary:** The boundary of $\Omega$ has no "infinitely sharp" cusps or fractal behaviour. Locally, it looks like the graph of a function with bounded slope. This assumption guarantees important results such as the trace theorem and the Poincaré inequality.
>
> **$W^{1,2}(\Omega)$:** The Sobolev space of functions whose values and first weak derivatives are square-integrable:
> $$
> W^{1,2}(\Omega)
> =
> \{u : u,\nabla u \in L^2(\Omega)\}.
> $$
> These functions have **finite Dirichlet energy**
> $$
> \int_\Omega |\nabla u|^2\,dx < \infty.
> $$
>
> **$W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$:** The subspace of $W^{1,2}(\Omega)$ consisting of functions whose trace is zero on the boundary:
> $$
> u=0 \quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.
> $$
>
> **$u_0+W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$:** The admissible set.
> Every function has the form
> $$
> u=u_0+v,\qquad v\in W_0^{1,2}(\Omega),
> $$
> so every admissible function satisfies the same boundary values as $u_0$.

Furthermore $\overline{u}$ satisfies the weak from of the lapace equation, namely

$$
(3.1) \qquad \int_\Omega \langle \nabla\overline{u}(x); \nabla \varphi(x) \rangle \, dx = 0, \forall \varphi \in W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)
$$

where $\langle .;. \rangle$ denotes the scalar product in $\mathbb{R}^n$ 
Conversely if $\overline{u} \in u_0 +W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$ satisfies $(3.1)$ then it is a minimiser of $(D)$

> [!intuition] Weak Laplace equation = "Derivative equals zero"
>
> In ordinary calculus:
>
> $$
> f'(x)=0
> $$
>
> characterises the stationary points of a function.
>
> In the calculus of variations, we minimise a **functional** instead of a number. The derivative becomes the **first variation**:
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}I(u+\varepsilon\varphi)\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}.
> $$
>
> Setting this equal to zero gives
>
> $$
> \int_\Omega
> \langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle\,dx=0
> \qquad
> \forall\varphi\in W_0^{1,2}(\Omega).
> $$
>
> This is called the **weak form of the Laplace equation**. It says that every admissible perturbation produces **zero first-order change** in the energy, just as $f'(x)=0$ does in ordinary calculus.

> [!tip] Recall: Scalar product (dot product)
>
> The scalar product
>
> $$
> \langle a,b\rangle
> $$
>
> is the ordinary dot product.
>
> In $\mathbb{R}^n$,
>
> $$
> \langle a,b\rangle
> =
> \sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> \langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle
> =
> \sum_{i=1}^n
> \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_i}
> \frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_i},
> $$
>
> and in one dimension this reduces to
>
> $$
> u'(x)\varphi'(x).
> $$
>
> **Remember:** the dot product measures how much two vectors point in the same direction. Here it compares the gradients of the solution and the test function.

### Remark 3.2
if $\overline{u} \in W^{2,2}(\Omega)$ then $(3.1)$ can be integrated by parts and we get,

$$
\int_\Omega \Delta\overline{u}(x)\varphi(x) \, dx = 0, \forall \varphi \in W_o^{1,2}(\Omega)
$$

> [!tip] Recall: The Laplacian
>
> The Laplacian is the sum of the second partial derivatives:
>
> $$
> \Delta u
> =
> u_{xx}+u_{yy}+u_{zz}
> \qquad
> (\text{or } \sum_{i=1}^n u_{x_ix_i} \text{ in } \mathbb{R}^n).
> $$
>
> It can also be written as
>
> $$
> \Delta u=\nabla\cdot(\nabla u),
> $$
>
> i.e. **the divergence of the gradient**.
>
> In the Direct Method, minimising
>
> $$
> \frac12\int_\Omega |\nabla u|^2\,dx
> $$
>
> leads to the weak equation
>
> $$
> \int_\Omega
> \langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle\,dx=0,
> $$
>
> which (for sufficiently smooth $u$) is equivalent to
>
> $$
> \boxed{\Delta u=0,}
> $$
>
> the **Laplace equation**.
> Intuitively, $\Delta u$ measures how much $u$ differs from the average of its nearby values.
