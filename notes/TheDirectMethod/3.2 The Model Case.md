
### Theorem 3.1
Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be bouned open set with Lipschitz boundary and $u_0 \in W^{1,2}(\Omega)$. The problem
$$
(D)\qquad \inf \left\{ I(u) = \frac{1}{2} \int_\Omega  |\nabla u(x)|^2 \, dx : u \in u_0+W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)\right\} = m
$$
has one and only one solution $\overline{u} \in u_0 + W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$.

> [!tip] Recall: What do these assumptions mean?
>
> **$\Omega$ bounded:** The domain has finite size, so integrals over $\Omega$ are finite.
>
> **Lipschitz boundary:** The boundary of $\Omega$ has no "infinitely sharp" cusps or fractal behaviour. Locally, it looks like the graph of a function with bounded slope. This assumption guarantees important results such as the trace theorem and the Poincaré inequality.
>
> **$W^{1,2}(\Omega)$:** The Sobolev space of functions whose values and first weak derivatives are square-integrable:
> $$
> W^{1,2}(\Omega)
> =
> \{u : u,\nabla u \in L^2(\Omega)\}.
> $$
> These functions have **finite Dirichlet energy**
> $$
> \int_\Omega |\nabla u|^2\,dx < \infty.
> $$
>
> **$W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$:** The subspace of $W^{1,2}(\Omega)$ consisting of functions whose trace is zero on the boundary:
> $$
> u=0 \quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.
> $$
>
> **$u_0+W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$:** The admissible set.
> Every function has the form
> $$
> u=u_0+v,\qquad v\in W_0^{1,2}(\Omega),
> $$
> so every admissible function satisfies the same boundary values as $u_0$.

Furthermore $\overline{u}$ satisfies the weak from of the lapace equation, namely

$$
(3.1) \qquad \int_\Omega \langle \nabla\overline{u}(x); \nabla \varphi(x) \rangle \, dx = 0, \forall \varphi \in W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)
$$

where $\langle .;. \rangle$ denotes the scalar product in $\mathbb{R}^n$ 
Conversely if $\overline{u} \in u_0 +W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$ satisfies $(3.1)$ then it is a minimiser of $(D)$

> [!intuition] Weak Laplace equation = "Derivative equals zero"
>
> In ordinary calculus:
>
> $$
> f'(x)=0
> $$
>
> characterises the stationary points of a function.
>
> In the calculus of variations, we minimise a **functional** instead of a number. The derivative becomes the **first variation**:
>
> $$
> \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}I(u+\varepsilon\varphi)\Big|_{\varepsilon=0}.
> $$
>
> Setting this equal to zero gives
>
> $$
> \int_\Omega
> \langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle\,dx=0
> \qquad
> \forall\varphi\in W_0^{1,2}(\Omega).
> $$
>
> This is called the **weak form of the Laplace equation**. It says that every admissible perturbation produces **zero first-order change** in the energy, just as $f'(x)=0$ does in ordinary calculus.

> [!tip] Recall: Scalar product (dot product)
>
> The scalar product
>
> $$
> \langle a,b\rangle
> $$
>
> is the ordinary dot product.
>
> In $\mathbb{R}^n$,
>
> $$
> \langle a,b\rangle
> =
> \sum_{i=1}^n a_ib_i.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
>
> $$
> \langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle
> =
> \sum_{i=1}^n
> \frac{\partial u}{\partial x_i}
> \frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_i},
> $$
>
> and in one dimension this reduces to
>
> $$
> u'(x)\varphi'(x).
> $$
>
> **Remember:** the dot product measures how much two vectors point in the same direction. Here it compares the gradients of the solution and the test function.

### Remark 3.2
if $\overline{u} \in W^{2,2}(\Omega)$ then $(3.1)$ can be integrated by parts and we get,

$$
\int_\Omega \Delta\overline{u}(x)\varphi(x) \, dx = 0, \forall \varphi \in W_o^{1,2}(\Omega)
$$

> [!tip] Recall: The Laplacian
>
> The Laplacian is the sum of the second partial derivatives:
>
> $$
> \Delta u
> =
> u_{xx}+u_{yy}+u_{zz}
> \qquad
> (\text{or } \sum_{i=1}^n u_{x_ix_i} \text{ in } \mathbb{R}^n).
> $$
>
> It can also be written as
>
> $$
> \Delta u=\nabla\cdot(\nabla u),
> $$
>
> i.e. **the divergence of the gradient**.
>
> In the Direct Method, minimising
>
> $$
> \frac12\int_\Omega |\nabla u|^2\,dx
> $$
>
> leads to the weak equation
>
> $$
> \int_\Omega
> \langle\nabla u,\nabla\varphi\rangle\,dx=0,
> $$
>
> which (for sufficiently smooth $u$) is equivalent to
>
> $$
> \boxed{\Delta u=0,}
> $$
>
> the **Laplace equation**.
> Intuitively, $\Delta u$ measures how much $u$ differs from the average of its nearby values.

### **Proof**
#### Part 1: Existence
##### Step 1: Compactness
Since $u_0 \in u_0 + W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$ we have
$$
	0 \le m \le I(u_0) \le \infty
$$

Let $u_\nu \in u_0 + W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$ be a minimising sequence of $(D)$, this means that

$$
I(u_\nu) \rightarrow \inf\{I(u)\} = m, \text{ as } \nu\rightarrow \infty
$$

Observe that by [[1.4 Sobolev Spaces#Theorem 1.47 (Poincaré inequality)|Poincaré inequality]] we can find constants $\gamma_1,\gamma_2>0$ so that,
$$
\sqrt{2I(u_\nu)} = \|\nabla u_\nu\|_{L^2} \ge \gamma_1\|u_\nu\|
_{W^{1,2}} - \gamma_2\|u_0\|_{W^{1,2}}
$$

Since $u_\nu$ is a minimising sequence and $m<\infty$ we deduced there exists $\gamma_3 > 0$ so that
$$
\|u_\nu\| \le \gamma_3
$$

it follows that there exists $\overline{u} \in u_0 + W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$ and a subsequence $u_\nu$ so that
$$
u_\nu \rightharpoonup \overline{u} \text{ in } W^{1,2}, \text{ as }v 
\rightarrow \infty
$$

> [!tip] Direct Method – Compactness Step (intuition)
>
> We define the energy functional:
>
> $$
> I(u)=\frac12\int_\Omega |\nabla u|^2\,dx
> $$
>
> This measures how “wiggly” a function is: large gradients ⇒ high energy.
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 1: Minimising sequence
> We choose a sequence \(u_\nu\) such that
>
> $$
> I(u_\nu)\to m=\inf I(u)
> $$
>
> meaning the energy becomes almost minimal.
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 2: Energy gives control
> Using the Poincaré inequality,
>
> $$
> \|u_\nu\|_{W^{1,2}} \le C(1 + I(u_\nu))
> $$
>
> so bounded energy ⇒ bounded Sobolev norm.
>
> **Interpretation:** low energy prevents wild oscillations.
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 3: Compactness (key idea)
> Since \(W^{1,2}(\Omega)\) is reflexive,
> every bounded sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence:
>
> $$
> u_\nu \rightharpoonup \overline{u} \quad \text{in } W^{1,2}(\Omega)
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Meaning of weak convergence
> The functions may not converge pointwise, but:
>
> $$
> \int_\Omega u_\nu \varphi \, dx \to \int_\Omega \overline{u}\varphi \, dx
> \quad \forall \varphi
> $$
>
> So the sequence stabilises when tested against all “probes” \(\varphi\).
>
> ---
>
> ### Big takeaway
> Minimising sequences do not converge directly —  
> but bounded energy forces compactness, and compactness gives a weak limit candidate \(\overline{u}\).

##### Step 2: Lower semicontinuity
We will show that $I$ is sequentially weakly lower semicontinuous; meaning, 

$$
u_\nu \rightharpoonup \overline{u} \text{ in } W^{1,2} \implies \lim_{\nu\rightarrow\infty} \inf I(u_\nu) \ge I(\overline{u})
$$

We trivially have
$$
\begin{aligned}
|\nabla u_\nu|^2 &= |\nabla \overline{u}|^2 + 2 \langle \nabla \overline{u}; \nabla u_\nu -  \nabla \overline{u}\rangle + |\nabla u_\nu -  \nabla \overline{u}|^2\\
&\ge |\nabla \overline{u}|^2 + 2 \langle \nabla \overline{u}; \nabla u_\nu -  \nabla \overline{u}\rangle
\end{aligned}
$$

Integrating this we have,
$$
I(u_\nu) \ge I(\overline{u}) + \int_\Omega \langle \nabla \overline{u}; \nabla u_\nu -  \nabla \overline{u} \rangle \, dx
$$
we need the 2nd term to tend to $0$. Since $\nabla \overline{u} \in L^2$ and $\nabla u_\nu -  \nabla \overline{u} \rightharpoonup 0$ in $L^2$ by definition of weak convergence in $L^2$, that
$$
\lim_{\nu\rightarrow\infty} \int_\Omega \langle \nabla \overline{u}; \nabla u_\nu -  \nabla \overline{u} \rangle \, dx = 0
$$

> [!tip] Expanding the gradient square (key Direct Method step)
>
> For vectors \(a,b \in \mathbb{R}^n\),
>
> $$
> |a|^2 = |b|^2 + 2\langle b, a-b\rangle + |a-b|^2
> $$
>
> Applying this with:
>
> $$
> a = \nabla u_v,\quad b = \nabla \overline{u}
> $$
>
> gives:
>
> $$
> |\nabla u_v|^2
> =
> |\nabla \overline{u}|^2
> +
> 2 \langle \nabla \overline{u}, \nabla u_v - \nabla \overline{u} \rangle
> +
> |\nabla u_v - \nabla \overline{u}|^2
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Key idea
> This splits the energy into:
>
> - limit part
> - linear interaction term
> - nonnegative error term
>
> ---
>
> ### Why it matters
> The last term is ≥ 0, which is crucial for proving:
>
> $$
> I(\overline{u}) \le \liminf I(u_v)
> $$
>
> (lower semicontinuity of the energy).

##### Step 3: Combination
Since $\{u_\nu\}$ was a minimising sequence (i.e. $I(u_\nu \rightarrow \inf\{I(u)\} =m$) and for such a sequence we have lower semicontinuity (i.e. $\lim \inf I(u_\nu) \ge I(\overline{u})$) we can deduce that $I(\overline{u}) =m$

#### Part 2: Uniqueness
Assume there exists $\overline{u},\overline{v} \in u_0 + W_0^{1,2}(\Omega)$ so that
$$
I(\overline{u}) = I(\overline{v}) = m
$$

I will omit the details but since the function $\xi \rightarrow |\xi|^2$ is convex then $\overline{u} = \overline{v}$.

#### Part 3: E-L equation
We want to establish  $(3.1)$. Let $\epsilon \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\varphi \in W^{0,1}_0(\Omega)$ be arbitrary. We note that $\overline{u} +\epsilon\varphi \in W^{0,1}_0(\Omega)$. Combined with $\overline{u}$ is minimiser of (D) leads to
$$
\begin{aligned}
I(\overline{u}) &\le I(\overline{u} +\epsilon\varphi)\\
&= \int_{\Omega} \frac{1}{2}|\nabla\overline{u} +\epsilon\nabla\varphi|^2 \, dx\\
&= I(\overline{u}) + \epsilon \int_{\Omega} \langle \nabla\overline{u};\nabla\varphi \rangle \, dx + \epsilon^2I(\varphi)
\end{aligned}
$$

As $\epsilon$ is arbitrary this leads to $(3.1)$ which expresses, 
$$
\left.\frac{d}{d\epsilon}I(\overline{u}+\epsilon\varphi)\right|_{\epsilon = 0} =0
$$
