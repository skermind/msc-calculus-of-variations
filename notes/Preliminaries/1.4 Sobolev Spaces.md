First we weaken the definition of the derivative
### Definition 1.26
let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^N$ be open and $u\in L^1_{loc}(\Omega)$ we say that $v\in L^1_{loc}(\Omega)$ is the **weak partial derivative** of u w.r.t. $x_i$ if
$$
\int_\Omega v(x)\,\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = - \int_\Omega \, u(x) \frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial x_i}\mathrm{d}x \quad \forall \varphi \in C^\infty_0(\Omega)
$$
allow $v = \delta u / \delta x_i$ or $u_{x_i}$.
We say that $u$ is **weakly differentiable ** if all weak partial derivatices, $u_{x_i}, ... , u_{u_n}$, exist.

> [!tip] Weak derivative example: $u(x)=|x|$
> **Goal:** Show that $u$ is weakly differentiable even though it is not classically differentiable at $x=0$.
>
> **Function:**
> $$
> u(x)=|x| \quad \text{on } (-1,1)
> $$
>
> Candidate weak derivative:
> $$
> v(x)=
> \begin{cases}
> -1, & x<0,\\
> 1, & x>0
> \end{cases}
> \quad \text{(value at } x=0 \text{ irrelevant)}
> $$
>
> So:
> $$
> v(x)=\operatorname{sgn}(x)
> $$
>
> **Weak derivative definition:**
> $$
> \int_{-1}^{1} v(x)\,\varphi(x)\,dx
> =
> -\int_{-1}^{1} |x|\,\varphi'(x)\,dx
> \quad \forall \varphi \in C_0^\infty(-1,1)
> $$
>
> **Key computation:**
>
> $$
> |x|=
> \begin{cases}
> -x, & x<0,\\
> x, & x>0
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> $$
> -\int_{-1}^{1} |x|\,\varphi'(x)\,dx
> =
> \int_{-1}^{0} x\,\varphi'(x)\,dx
> -
> \int_{0}^{1} x\,\varphi'(x)\,dx
> $$
>
> Integration by parts:
>
> $$
> \int_{-1}^{0} x\,\varphi'(x)\,dx
> =
> [x\varphi(x)]_{-1}^{0}
> -
> \int_{-1}^{0} \varphi(x)\,dx
> =
> -\int_{-1}^{0} \varphi(x)\,dx
> $$
>
> $$
> -\int_{0}^{1} x\,\varphi'(x)\,dx
> =
> \int_{0}^{1} \varphi(x)\,dx
> $$
>
> Combine:
>
> $$
> -\int_{-1}^{1} |x|\,\varphi'(x)\,dx
> =
> -\int_{-1}^{0} \varphi(x)\,dx
> +
> \int_{0}^{1} \varphi(x)\,dx
> $$
>
> Recognise:
>
> $$
> = \int_{-1}^{1} \operatorname{sgn}(x)\,\varphi(x)\,dx
> $$
>
> **Conclusion:**
> $$
> u_x = \operatorname{sgn}(x)
> $$
>
> **Key insight:** Weak derivatives extend differentiation to non-smooth functions.\

### Definition 1.31
let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be an open set and $1 \le p \le \infty$.
(i) We let $W^{1,p}(\Omega)$ be the set of functions $u : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$, $u \in L^P(\Omega)$, whose weak partial derivatives $u_{x_i} \in L^p(\Omega)$ for every $i=1,...,n$. This space has the following norm,
$$
\|u\|_{W^{1,p}} = (\|u\|^p_{L^p} + \|\nabla u\|^p_{l^p})^{\frac{1}{p}} 
$$
$$
\|u\|_{W^{1,\infty}} = \max\{\|u\|_{L^\infty}, \|\nabla u\|_{l^\infty}\}
$$
in the case of $p=2$ the space $W^{1,2}(\Omega)$ is somteimews denoted by $H^1(\Omega)$.

> [!example] Sobolev norm example: computing in $W^{1,p}$
>
> Let $\Omega = (-1,1)$ and $u(x)=|x|$.
>
> We already know:
> $$
> u_x = \operatorname{sgn}(x)
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Case 1: $W^{1,1}$
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{W^{1,1}} = \|u\|_{L^1} + \|u_x\|_{L^1}
> $$
>
> Compute each part:
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{L^1} = \int_{-1}^1 |x|\,dx = 2\int_0^1 x\,dx = 1
> $$
>
> $$
> \|u_x\|_{L^1} = \int_{-1}^1 |\operatorname{sgn}(x)|\,dx = \int_{-1}^1 1\,dx = 2
> $$
>
> So:
> $$
> \|u\|_{W^{1,1}} = 3
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Case 2: $W^{1,2} = H^1$
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{W^{1,2}} = \left(\|u\|_{L^2}^2 + \|u_x\|_{L^2}^2\right)^{1/2}
> $$
>
> Compute:
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{L^2}^2 = \int_{-1}^1 x^2\,dx = \frac{2}{3}
> $$
>
> $$
> \|u_x\|_{L^2}^2 = \int_{-1}^1 1\,dx = 2
> $$
>
> So:
> $$
> \|u\|_{W^{1,2}} = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3} + 2} = \sqrt{\frac{8}{3}}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Why is $p=2$ called $H^1$?
>
> When $p=2$, the norm comes from an inner product:
>
> $$
> \langle u,v \rangle_{H^1}
> =
> \int_\Omega u v \,dx + \int_\Omega \nabla u \cdot \nabla v \,dx
> $$
>
> This makes $W^{1,2}(\Omega)$ a **Hilbert space**, meaning:
>
> - it has an inner product
> - geometry works like Euclidean space
> - projection methods and Fourier methods apply
>
> So we rename:
> $$
> W^{1,2}(\Omega) = H^1(\Omega)
> $$
>
> **Key idea:**  
> $p=2$ is special because it gives a geometric structure (angles, orthogonality), not just a norm.

(iii) The set $W_0^{1,p}(\Omega)$ is the closure of $C_0^\infty(\Omega)$ functions in $W^{1,p}(\Omega)$. If $\Omega$ is bounded then $u \in W_0^{1,p}(\Omega)$ is such that $u \in W^{1,p}(\Omega)$ and $u=0$ on $\delta\Omega$.

> [!tip] Meaning of $W^{1,p}_0(\Omega)$: boundary condition in Sobolev sense
>
> $W^{1,p}_0(\Omega)$ is defined as the **closure of $C^\infty_0(\Omega)$** in the $W^{1,p}$-norm.
>
> This means:
>
> - start with smooth functions that are zero near the boundary  
> - take limits in the Sobolev norm  
> - the limit functions form $W^{1,p}_0(\Omega)$
>
> ---
>
> ### Key idea
>
> $$
> W^{1,p}_0(\Omega) \approx \{ u \in W^{1,p}(\Omega) : u = 0 \text{ on } \partial\Omega \}
> $$
>
> (this is in the **trace sense**, not pointwise)
>
> ---
>
> ### Example intuition
>
> Let $\Omega = (-1,1)$.
>
> The function
>
> $$
> u(x) = 1
> $$
>
> is in $W^{1,p}(\Omega)$, but:
>
> - it does NOT vanish at the boundary  
> - so it is NOT in $W^{1,p}_0(\Omega)$
>
> ---
>
> Now take:
>
> $$
> u(x) = 1 - x^2
> $$
>
> Then:
>
> - $u \in W^{1,p}(\Omega)$  
> - $u(\pm 1)=0$  
> - so $u \in W^{1,p}_0(\Omega)$
>
> ---
>
> ### Why this matters
>
> $$
> u = 0 \quad \text{on } \partial\Omega
> $$
>
> is the natural Dirichlet boundary condition in PDEs.
>
> ---
>
> ### Key takeaway
>
> $W^{1,p}_0(\Omega)$ = Sobolev functions approximable by smooth functions that vanish at the boundary.

(iv) $u \in u_0 + W^{1,p}_0(\Omega)$ means $u,u_0 \in W^{1,p}(\Omega)$ and $u-u_0 \in W^{1,p}_0(\Omega)$.

(v) $W^{1,\infty}_0 = W^{1,\infty} \cap W^{1,1}_0$ 

(iv) For higher derivatives we define Sobolev spaces as  follows. For $k \in \mathbb{N}, \quad k \ge 1$, $W^{k,p}$ is the set of fucntions whose weak partial dervatives $D^{a}u \in L^p$ for every multi-index $a\in\mathcal{A}_m$, $0\le m \le k$. The norm would be,
For $1\le p<\infty$
$$
\|u\|_{W^{k,p}} = \left( \sum_{0\le|a|\le k} \|D^{a}u\|^p_{L^p} \right)^\frac{1}{p}.
$$
For $p=\infty$
$$
\|u\|_{W^{k,p}} = \max_{0\le|a|\le k}(\|D^{a}u\|_{L^\infty}).
$$
> [!tip] Meaning of $D^\alpha u$ (multi-index derivatives in Sobolev spaces)
>
> In Sobolev spaces, $D^\alpha u$ means a **mixed weak partial derivative** of order $|\alpha|$.
>
> ---
>
> ## What is a multi-index $\alpha$?
>
> A multi-index is:
>
> $$
> \alpha = (\alpha_1, \dots, \alpha_n), \quad \alpha_i \in \mathbb{N}
> $$
>
> Its order is:
>
> $$
> |\alpha| = \alpha_1 + \cdots + \alpha_n
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Definition of $D^\alpha$
>
> $$
> D^\alpha u
> =
> \frac{\partial^{|\alpha|} u}
> {\partial x_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots \partial x_n^{\alpha_n}}
> $$
>
> So $D^\alpha u$ means:
>
> - derivatives in each coordinate direction
> - possibly mixed derivatives (like $x$ and $y$ together)
>
> ---
>
> ## Example in $\mathbb{R}^2$
>
> Let $u(x,y)=x^2y$.
>
> Take multi-index:
>
> $$
> \alpha = (1,1)
> $$
>
> Then:
>
> $$
> D^\alpha u = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x \partial y}
> $$
>
> Compute step by step:
>
> - first in $x$: $u_x = 2xy$
> - then in $y$: $(2xy)_y = 2x$
>
> So:
>
> $$
> D^{(1,1)}u = 2x
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Another example
>
> If:
>
> $$
> \alpha = (2,0)
> $$
>
> then:
>
> $$
> D^\alpha u = \frac{\partial^2 u}{\partial x^2}
> $$
>
> For $u(x,y)=x^2y$:
>
> $$
> u_{xx} = 2y
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Key idea
>
> $D^\alpha u$ is just a compact way of writing:
>
> - first derivatives
> - second derivatives
> - mixed derivatives
>
> all in one unified notation
>
> ---
>
> ## Sobolev meaning
>
> $u \in W^{k,p}(\Omega)$ means:
>
> $$
> D^\alpha u \in L^p(\Omega) \quad \text{for all } |\alpha| \le k
> $$

> [!tip] Example: computing a Sobolev norm in $W^{1,2}(\Omega)$
>
> Let $\Omega = (-1,1)$ and $u(x)=x^2$.
>
> We compute the $W^{1,2}$ norm:
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{W^{1,2}}^2
> =
> \|u\|_{L^2}^2 + \|u'\|_{L^2}^2
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 1: compute $u$ and its derivative
>
> $$
> u(x)=x^2, \quad u'(x)=2x
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 2: compute $L^2$ norms
>
> ### Function term
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{L^2}^2
> =
> \int_{-1}^1 x^4\,dx
> =
> \left[\frac{x^5}{5}\right]_{-1}^1
> =
> \frac{2}{5}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Derivative term
>
> $$
> \|u'\|_{L^2}^2
> =
> \int_{-1}^1 (2x)^2\,dx
> =
> \int_{-1}^1 4x^2\,dx
> =
> 4\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_{-1}^1
> =
> \frac{8}{3}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 3: combine
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{W^{1,2}}^2
> =
> \frac{2}{5} + \frac{8}{3}
> =
> \frac{46}{15}
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{W^{1,2}} = \sqrt{\frac{46}{15}}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Key idea
>
> The $W^{k,p}$ norm is just:
>
> - take all weak derivatives up to order $k$
> - take their $L^p$ norms
> - combine them into one “energy-like” quantity
> > [!tip] Example: computing a $W^{2,2}(\Omega)$ norm (i.e. $H^2$ norm)
>
> Let $\Omega = (-1,1)$ and $u(x)=x^2$.
>
> We compute the $W^{2,2}$ norm:
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{W^{2,2}}^2
> =
> \|u\|_{L^2}^2 + \|u'\|_{L^2}^2 + \|u''\|_{L^2}^2
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 1: derivatives
>
> $$
> u(x)=x^2, \quad u'(x)=2x, \quad u''(x)=2
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 2: compute each term
>
> ### (1) Function term
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{L^2}^2
> =
> \int_{-1}^1 x^4\,dx
> =
> \frac{2}{5}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### (2) First derivative term
>
> $$
> \|u'\|_{L^2}^2
> =
> \int_{-1}^1 (2x)^2\,dx
> =
> \int_{-1}^1 4x^2\,dx
> =
> \frac{8}{3}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### (3) Second derivative term
>
> $$
> \|u''\|_{L^2}^2
> =
> \int_{-1}^1 4\,dx
> =
> 8
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 3: combine
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{W^{2,2}}^2
> =
> \frac{2}{5} + \frac{8}{3} + 8
> =
> \frac{274}{15}
> $$
>
> so
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{W^{2,2}} = \sqrt{\frac{274}{15}}
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Key idea
>
> A $W^{2,2}$ norm measures:
>
> - size of the function
> - size of slope
> - size of curvature
>
> all in an $L^2$ (energy) sense

> [!tip] Key insight: roles of $k$ and $p$ in Sobolev spaces (C.o.V. perspective)
>
> In Sobolev spaces $W^{k,p}(\Omega)$, the two parameters have very different roles:
>
> ---
>
> ## Role of $k$ (smoothness level)
>
> $k$ controls **how many weak derivatives exist**.
>
> $$
> u \in W^{k,p}(\Omega)
> \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
> D^\alpha u \in L^p(\Omega)\ \text{for all } |\alpha|\le k
> $$
>
> **Meaning:**
> - $k=0$: just integrability
> - $k=1$: control of slope
> - $k=2$: control of curvature
> - higher $k$: higher-order regularity
>
> So:
> > $k$ = *what you measure (order of differentiation)*
>
> ---
>
> ## Role of $p$ (how you measure size)
>
> $p$ controls **how strongly large values are penalised**:
>
> $$
> \|f\|_{L^p} = \left(\int_\Omega |f|^p \, dx\right)^{1/p}
> $$
>
> **Meaning:**
> - small $p$ (e.g. $p=1$): allows more irregularity, weak control
> - large $p$: strongly penalises spikes
> - $p=\infty$: controls only the maximum value
>
> So:
> > $p$ = *how strict the notion of size is*
>
> ---
>
> ## Why $p=2$ is special (calculus of variations)
>
> In most C.o.V. problems, the energy is quadratic, e.g.
>
> $$
> E(u)=\int_\Omega |\nabla u|^2 \, dx
> $$
>
> This leads naturally to:
> - $L^2$ norms
> - $W^{k,2}$ spaces
> - inner product structure
>
> So:
> $$
> W^{k,2} = H^k
> $$
>
> and in particular:
> - $H^1$ is the standard space for variational problems
>
> ---
>
> ## Why this matters
>
> - $p=2$ gives a **Hilbert space structure**
> - enables geometry (angles, projections, orthogonality)
> - simplifies Euler–Lagrange theory
>
> ---
>
> ## Key takeaway
>
> - $k$ = *how many derivatives you control*
> - $p$ = *how strongly you penalise size*
> - $p=2$ = *natural setting for energy minimisation and PDEs*
>
> ---
>
> ## One-line intuition
>
> $$
> \text{C.o.V. typically lives in } H^k(\Omega)=W^{k,2}(\Omega)
> \text{ because energies are quadratic.}
> $$

### Theorem 1.47 (Poincaré inequality)

There exists $\gamma = \gamma(\Omega,p) > 0$  so that, 

$$
\|u\|_{L^p \text{ or } W^{1,p}} \le \gamma\|\nabla u\|_{L^p}
\, \quad \forall u \in W^{1,p}_0$$

> [!tip] Poincaré inequality intuition + example
>
> The Poincaré inequality says:
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{L^p(\Omega)} \le \gamma(\Omega,p)\,\|\nabla u\|_{L^p(\Omega)}
> \quad \forall u \in W^{1,p}_0(\Omega)
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 🧠 Intuition table
>
> | Object | Meaning | Intuition |
> |--------|--------|-----------|
> | $u$ | function size | how big the function is |
> | $\nabla u$ | gradient | how steep it is |
> | $\gamma$ | constant | how much the domain allows growth |
>
> ---
>
> ## 🔑 Key idea
>
> A function in $W^{1,p}_0(\Omega)$ is zero on the boundary, so:
>
> > it can only become large by accumulating slope.
>
> ---
>
> ## 📌 Example in 1D
>
> Let $\Omega = (0,L)$ and take:
>
> $$
> u(x) = x(L-x)
> $$
>
> Then:
>
> - $u(0)=u(L)=0$
> - so $u \in W^{1,p}_0(\Omega)$
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 1: derivative
>
> $$
> u'(x) = L - 2x
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## Step 2: Poincaré inequality
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{L^p} \le \gamma \|u'\|_{L^p}
> $$
>
> meaning:
>
> > total size of the function is controlled by its slope
>
> ---
>
> ## 📈 Scaling intuition
>
> If we increase the domain size $L$:
>
> - $u$ grows like $L^2$
> - $u'$ grows like $L$
>
> so:
>
> $$
> \frac{\|u\|}{\|u'\|} \sim L
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ## 🔥 Meaning of $\gamma$
>
> - small domain → $\gamma \approx 1$
> - large domain → $\gamma$ large
>
> So:
>
> > $\gamma$ measures how much “room” the domain gives a function to grow from zero.
>
> ---
>
> ## ⚡ Key takeaway
>
> $$
> \text{A function cannot be large unless its gradient is large somewhere.}
> $$
