### Definition 1.11
let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be an open set and $1 \le p \le \infty$.  We say that a measureable function $u : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ belongs to $l^p(\Omega)$ if $\|u\|_{L^p}$ is finite. For $p$ finite
$$
\|u\|_{L^p} = \left(\int_\Omega |u(x)|^p \, dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
$$
And for $p = \infty$
$$
\|u\|_{L^p} = \inf\{\alpha : |u(x)<\alpha \text{ a.e. in } \Omega|\}
$$

> [!example] Examples of $L^p$ Norms
>
> **(i) Case $p<\infty$**
>
> Let $\Omega=(0,1)$ and define
> $$
> u(x)=x.
> $$
>
> For $p=2$,
> $$
> \|u\|_{L^2}
> =
> \left(\int_0^1 x^2\,dx\right)^{1/2}
> =
> \left(\frac13\right)^{1/2}
> =
> \frac{1}{\sqrt3}.
> $$
>
> Since the norm is finite, $u\in L^2(0,1)$.
>
> ---
>
> **(ii) Case $p=\infty$**
>
> Let $\Omega=(0,1)$ and define
> $$
> u(x)=x.
> $$
>
> The largest value attained by $u$ on $(0,1)$ is arbitrarily close to $1$, so
> $$
> \|u\|_{L^\infty}
> =
> \operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x\in(0,1)} |u(x)|
> =
> 1.
> $$
>
> Since the essential supremum is finite, $u\in L^\infty(0,1)$.

> [!example] A Function in $L^1$ but not in $L^2$
>
> Let
> $$
> u(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}
> $$
> on $\Omega=(0,1)$.
>
> Then
> $$
> \|u\|_{L^1}
> =
> \int_0^1 x^{-1/2}\,dx
> =
> 2,
> $$
>
> so $u\in L^1(0,1)$.
>
> However,
> $$
> \int_0^1 |u(x)|^2\,dx
> =
> \int_0^1 x^{-1}\,dx
> =
> \infty,
> $$
>
> and therefore
> $$
> u\notin L^2(0,1).
> $$

### Theorem 1.13
let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be an open set and $1 \le p \le \infty$.
(i) $L^p(\Omega)$ is a Banach Space. The space **$l^2(\Omega)$** is a **Hilbert Space** with scalar product given by,
$$
\langle u;v\rangle = \int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x) dx
$$
 
(ii) **Hölder inequality** assets that if $u \in L^p$ and $v \in L^{p'}$ where,
$$
\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{p'} = 1, \qquad \text{i.e., }  p' = \frac{p}{(p-1)}
$$
Then  $uv \in L^1(\Omega)$  and
$$
\|uv\|_{L^1} \le \|u\|_{L^p}\|v\|_{L^{p'}}
$$
In the case of $p,p' = 2$ Then,
$$
\|uv\|_{L^1} \le \|u\|_{L^2}\|v\|_{L^{2}}
$$
(iii) **Minkowski inequality** asserts that
$$
\|uv\|_{L^p} \le \|u\|_{L^p}\|v\|_{L^p}
$$
(iv)**Riesz Theorem:** the dual space of $L^p$, denoted by $(L^p)'$. If $p$ is finite. If $\varphi in (L^p)'$ there exisist a unique $u \in L^{p'}$ so that,
$$
\langle \varphi;f \rangle = \varphi(f) = \int_{\Omega} u(x)\,f(x)\,dx, \forall f \in L^{p}(\Omega)
$$

> [!tip] Riesz Representation Theorem (Duality in $L^p$)
>
> ### What is a dual space?
>
> Let $X$ be a normed vector space. The **dual space** $X'$ is the set of all **continuous linear functionals**
> $$
> \varphi : X \to \mathbb{R}.
> $$
>
> So elements of the dual space are not functions *in* $X$, but **rules that take functions in $X$ and return numbers**.
>
> ---
>
> ### Riesz Representation Theorem ($L^p$ version)
>
> If $1 < p < \infty$ and $p'$ satisfies
> $$
> \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{p'} = 1,
> $$
> then every $\varphi \in (L^p(\Omega))'$ can be written uniquely as
> $$
> \varphi(f) = \int_{\Omega} u(x)\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x
> $$
> for some unique $u \in L^{p'}(\Omega)$.
>
> In short:
> $$
> (L^p)' \cong L^{p'}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Intuition
>
> - Elements of $L^p$ are functions $f$
> - Elements of $(L^p)'$ are “measurement devices” that assign numbers to $f$
> - The theorem says every such measurement device comes from **integration against another function**
>
> ---
>
> ### Example (how it is used)
>
> Let $\Omega = (0,1)$ and $p=2$.
>
> Then $p'=2$, so $(L^2)' = L^2$.
>
> Define a functional:
> $$
> \varphi(f) = \int_0^1 x\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.
> $$
>
> Here:
> $$
> u(x) = x \in L^2(0,1).
> $$
>
> So $\varphi$ is an element of the dual space $(L^2)'$.
>
> ---
>
> ### Why this matters in Calculus of Variations
>
> In variational problems, we often define functionals like
> $$
> I(f) = \int_\Omega g(x)\,f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.
> $$
>
> Riesz tells us:
>
> > every linear functional on $L^p$ comes from integration against a function in $L^{p'}$.
>
> This is the foundation of:
> - weak formulations of PDEs
> - Euler–Lagrange equations
> - distribution theory ideas

> [!example] Approximation in $L^p$ by smooth compactly supported functions
>
> Let $\Omega = (0,1)$ and define
> $$
> u(x) = 1 \quad \text{for } x \in (0,1).
> $$
> Then $u \in L^p(0,1)$ for every $1 \le p < \infty$.
>
> ---
>
> ### Construction of smooth approximation
>
> Define a sequence $(u_\nu) \subset C_0^\infty(0,1)$ such that:
>
> - $u_\nu(x) = 1$ for $x \in (2/\nu,\,1-2/\nu)$,
> - $u_\nu(x) = 0$ near the boundary $(0,1/\nu) \cup (1-1/\nu,1)$,
> - $u_\nu$ is smoothly interpolated between $0$ and $1$.
>
> ---
>
> ### Convergence in $L^p$, $1 \le p < \infty$
>
> The difference $u_\nu - u$ is supported only near the boundary, on a set of size $\sim 1/\nu$. Hence
> $$
> \|u_\nu - u\|_{L^p}^p
> \le \int_0^{1/\nu} |1|^p\,dx + \int_{1-1/\nu}^{1} |1|^p\,dx
> = \frac{2}{\nu}.
> $$
>
> Therefore,
> $$
> \|u_\nu - u\|_{L^p}
> \le \left(\frac{2}{\nu}\right)^{1/p} \to 0.
> $$
>
> So,
> $$
> u_\nu \to u \quad \text{in } L^p(0,1).
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Failure in $L^\infty$
>
> In the supremum norm,
> $$
> \|u_\nu - u\|_{L^\infty}
> =
> \operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x \in (0,1)} |u_\nu(x) - 1|
> = 1
> $$
> for all $\nu$, since $u_\nu$ differs from $u$ on a set of positive measure.
>
> Hence,
> $$
> \|u_\nu - u\|_{L^\infty} \nrightarrow 0.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Key insight
>
> - In $L^p$ ($p<\infty$), small sets have small effect.
> - In $L^\infty$, any nonzero pointwise error prevents convergence.
>

### Remark 1.14
Summarise the results on duality we have, 
 $$
(L^p)' = L^{p'} \quad \text{if } 1 < p < \infty
$$
$$
(L^2)' = L^2
$$
$$
(L^1)' = L^\infty
$$
$$
L^1 \subsetneq (L^\infty)'
$$
### Definition 1.15 
Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be an open set and $1\le p \le \infty$
(i) A sequence $u_{\mu}$ is said to  (**Strongly**) converge to u if $u_{\mu}$, $u \in L^p$ and if
$$
\lim_{\nu \rightarrow \infty}\|u_\nu - u \|_{L^p} = 0
$$
denoted by $u_\nu \rightarrow u$ in $L^p$

(ii) If $1\le p < \infty$, the sequence $u_{\mu}$ is said to  (**weakly**) convergages to u if $u_{\mu}$, $u \in L^p$ and if
$$
\lim_{\nu \rightarrow \infty} \int_\Omega [u_{\nu}(x) - u(x)]\, \varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 \,, \forall \varphi \in L^{p'}(\Omega).
$$
denoted by $u_\nu \rightharpoonup u$ in $L^p$

> [!example] Weak convergence in $L^p$
>
> Let $\Omega = (0,1)$ and define
> $$
> u_n(x) = \sin(2\pi n x).
> $$
>
> Then for every $\varphi \in L^{p'}(0,1)$,
> $$
> \int_0^1 (u_n(x) - 0)\,\varphi(x)\,dx
> =
> \int_0^1 \sin(2\pi n x)\,\varphi(x)\,dx
> \to 0.
> $$
>
> Hence,
> $$
> u_n \rightharpoonup 0 \quad \text{in } L^p(0,1), \; 1 \le p < \infty.
> $$
>
> However, $\|u_n\|_{L^p}$ does not go to $0$, so convergence is not strong.

(iii) if $p = \infty$, the sequence $u_{\mu}$ is said to  **weak-$*$**  converge to u if $u_{\mu}$, $u \in L^\infty$ and if
$$
\lim_{\nu \rightarrow \infty} \int_\Omega [u_{\nu}(x) - u(x)]\, \varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = 0 \,, \forall \varphi \in L^{1}(\Omega).
$$
denoted by $u_\nu \rightharpoonup^* u$ in $L^\infty$

> [!example] Weak-* convergence in $L^\infty$
>
> Let $\Omega = (0,1)$ and define a bounded oscillating sequence $(u_n) \subset L^\infty(\Omega)$, for example
> piecewise alternating functions taking values $\pm 1$ on finer and finer partitions of $(0,1)$.
>
> Then $\|u_n\|_{L^\infty} \le 1$, so the sequence is bounded in $L^\infty$.
>
> For every $\varphi \in L^1(\Omega)$,
> $$
> \int_\Omega u_n(x)\,\varphi(x)\,\mathrm{d}x \to 0.
> $$
>
> Hence,
> $$
> u_n \rightharpoonup^* 0 \quad \text{in } L^\infty(\Omega).
> $$
>
> However, $u_n$ does not converge strongly in $L^\infty$.


> [!intuition] Relationships between strong, weak, and weak-* convergence
>
> ### Always true:
>
> **1. Strong ⇒ Weak**
> $$
> u_n \to u \text{ in } L^p \;\Rightarrow\; u_n \rightharpoonup u \text{ in } L^p
> $$
>
> **Intuition:**  
> If functions are close in *size everywhere on average* (strong convergence), then they must give almost the same result when tested against any fixed function $\varphi$. So all “measurements” agree in the limit.
>
> ---
>
> **2. Strong in $L^\infty$ ⇒ Weak-***
> $$
> u_n \to u \text{ in } L^\infty \;\Rightarrow\; u_n \rightharpoonup^* u \text{ in } L^\infty
> $$
>
> **Intuition:**  
> If functions converge in the supremum norm, then they are uniformly close everywhere. Therefore, when integrated against any $\varphi \in L^1$, the difference becomes negligible, so all $L^1$-based measurements converge.
>
> ---
>
> ### Big picture:
>
> - **Strong convergence** controls actual size of the function.
> - **Weak convergence** only controls averages against test functions.
> - **Weak-* convergence** is the same idea, but adapted to $L^\infty$ where testing is done with $L^1$ functions.

### Definition 1.23 
$u \in L^p_{loc}(\Omega)$ if $u \in L^p(\Omega')$ for every open set $\Omega'$ ompactly contained in $\Omega$

> [!example] Local $L^p$ functions
>
> Let $\Omega = (0,1)$.
>
> ---
>
> ### Example 1: globally well-behaved function
>
> Let
> $$
> u(x) = x.
> $$
>
> Then for any $\Omega' \Subset (0,1)$ (for example $\Omega' = (0.2,0.8)$),
> $u$ is bounded on $\Omega'$, hence
> $$
> u \in L^p(\Omega')
> \quad \forall\, 1 \le p < \infty.
> $$
>
> So:
> $$
> u \in L^p_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,1).
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Example 2: singular near the boundary
>
> Let
> $$
> u(x) = \frac{1}{x}.
> $$
>
> This function is not in $L^p(0,1)$ for large $p$ due to the blow-up at $x=0$.
>
> However, if we take any compactly contained subinterval, e.g.
> $$
> \Omega' = (0.2,0.8) \Subset (0,1),
> $$
> then $u$ is bounded on $\Omega'$ and hence
> $$
> u \in L^p(\Omega').
> $$
>
> Therefore:
> $$
> u \in L^p_{\mathrm{loc}}(0,1).
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Key takeaway
>
> A function can fail to be globally in $L^p(\Omega)$ due to boundary blow-up, but still belong to $L^p_{\mathrm{loc}}(\Omega)$ because it behaves well on every region strictly inside $\Omega$.

### Theorem 1.24 (Fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations).

Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be an open set and $u \in L^1_{loc}(\Omega)$ be such that 
$$
\int_\Omega u(x)\, \varphi(x) \, \mathrm{d}x = 0, \qquad \forall \varphi \in C^{\infty}_0(\Omega)
$$
then $u = 0$, a.e. in $\Omega$.

### Corollary 1.25
from above, if
$$
\int_\Omega \varphi(x) \,\mathrm{d}x = 0
$$
then $u=\text{constant}$, a.e. $\Omega$
