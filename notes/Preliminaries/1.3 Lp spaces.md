### Definition 1.11
let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be an open set and $1 \le p \le \infty$.  We say that a measureable function $u : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ belongs to $l^p(\Omega)$ if $\|u\|_{L^p}$ is finite. For $p$ finite
$$
\|u\|_{L^p} = \left(\int_\Omega |u(x)|^p \, dx\right)^{\frac{1}{p}}
$$
And for $p = \infty$
$$
\|u\|_{L^p} = \inf\{\alpha : |u(x)<\alpha \text{ a.e. in } \Omega|\}
$$

> [!example] Examples of $L^p$ Norms
>
> **(i) Case $p<\infty$**
>
> Let $\Omega=(0,1)$ and define
> $$
> u(x)=x.
> $$
>
> For $p=2$,
> $$
> \|u\|_{L^2}
> =
> \left(\int_0^1 x^2\,dx\right)^{1/2}
> =
> \left(\frac13\right)^{1/2}
> =
> \frac{1}{\sqrt3}.
> $$
>
> Since the norm is finite, $u\in L^2(0,1)$.
>
> ---
>
> **(ii) Case $p=\infty$**
>
> Let $\Omega=(0,1)$ and define
> $$
> u(x)=x.
> $$
>
> The largest value attained by $u$ on $(0,1)$ is arbitrarily close to $1$, so
> $$
> \|u\|_{L^\infty}
> =
> \operatorname*{ess\,sup}_{x\in(0,1)} |u(x)|
> =
> 1.
> $$
>
> Since the essential supremum is finite, $u\in L^\infty(0,1)$.

> [!example] A Function in $L^1$ but not in $L^2$
>
> Let
> $$
> u(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}
> $$
> on $\Omega=(0,1)$.
>
> Then
> $$
> \|u\|_{L^1}
> =
> \int_0^1 x^{-1/2}\,dx
> =
> 2,
> $$
>
> so $u\in L^1(0,1)$.
>
> However,
> $$
> \int_0^1 |u(x)|^2\,dx
> =
> \int_0^1 x^{-1}\,dx
> =
> \infty,
> $$
>
> and therefore
> $$
> u\notin L^2(0,1).
> $$

### Theorem 1.13
let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be an open set and $1 \le p \le \infty$.
(i) $L^p(\Omega)$ is a Banach Space. The space **$l^2(\Omega)$** is a **Hilbert Space** with scalar product given by,
$$
\langle u;v\rangle = \int_{\Omega}u(x)v(x) dx
$$
 
(ii) **Hölder inequality** assets that if $u \in L^p$ and $v \in L^{p'}$ where,
$$
\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{p'} = 1, \qquad \text{i.e., }  p' = \frac{p}{(p-1)}
$$
Then  $uv \in L^1(\Omega)$  and
$$
\|uv\|_{L^1} \le \|u\|_{L^p}\|v\|_{L^{p'}}
$$
In the case of $p,p' = 2$ Then,
$$
\|uv\|_{L^1} \le \|u\|_{L^2}\|v\|_{L^{2}}
$$
> [!tip] Support of a function — intuition + example
>
> The support of a function is the closure of the region where the function is *not zero*.
>
> ### Example
> Define:
> $$
> u(x) =
> \begin{cases}
> 1 - x^2 & \text{if } x \in (-1,1) \\
> 0 & \text{otherwise}
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Then:
> - $u(x) \neq 0$ only for $x \in (-1,1)$
> - so the set $\{x \in \Omega : u(x) \neq 0\} = (-1,1)$
> - and the support is:
> $$
> \operatorname{supp}(u) = \overline{(-1,1)} = [-1,1]
> $$
>
> ### Key idea
> The support is the *closure* of where the function lives — it includes boundary points even if the function is zero there.

(iv) $C_0(\Omega) = \{u \in C(\Omega) : \operatorname{supp} u \subset \Omega \text{ is compact}\}$

> [!tip] Example intuition for $C_0(\Omega)$ (Vanish at Infinity)
>
> Take $\Omega = (-2,2)$ and define
> $$
> u(x) =
> \begin{cases}
> 1 - x^2 & \text{if } |x| < 1 \\
> 0 & \text{if } 1 \le |x| < 2
> \end{cases}
> $$
>
> Then:
> - $u(x)$ is continuous on $\Omega$
> - $u(x) \neq 0$ only on $(-1,1)$
> - so
> $$
> \operatorname{supp}(u) = [-1,1]
> $$
>
> 💡 Key idea: even though \(\Omega\) is bigger, the function “lives” only inside a smaller compact region and is zero elsewhere.

(v) We define the norm over $C(\overline{\Omega})$, $$ \|u\|_{C^0}
= \sup_{x \in \overline{\Omega}} |u(x)|$$
> [!tip] Supremum norm intuition
>
> The norm
> $$
> \|u\|_{C^0(\overline{\Omega})}
> = \sup_{x \in \overline{\Omega}} |u(x)|
> $$
> measures the **maximum height of the function** on the domain.
>
> 💡 Think: “the worst-case value of \(u\) anywhere in the domain.”
>
> ### Example
> If $u(x)=x^2$ on $[-1,1]$, then:
> - the maximum value is $1$
> - so $\|u\|_{C^0}=1$

### Remark 1.2 
$C(\overline{\Omega)})$ equipped with the norm $\|\cdot \|_{C^0}$ is **Banach Space**
### Theorem 1.3 (Ascoli-Arzela Theorem)) 
Let $\Omega \in \mathbb{R}^n$ be a bounded domain. Let $K \subset C(\overline{\Omega)}$ be bounded such that **equicontinuity** holds. $\forall \varepsilon >0$, there exsists $\delta > 0$ 
$$|x-y| < \delta \implies |u(x) - u(y)| < \varepsilon, \forall x,y \in \overline{\Omega} \text{ and } \forall u\in K $$  
Then $\overline{K} is compact
> [!tip] Intuition for Arzelà–Ascoli
>
> ## Example (before the theorem)
>
> Consider the sequence of functions on $[0,1]$:
> $$
> u_n(x) = x^n
> $$
>
> - Each $u_n$ is continuous on $[0,1]$
> - All functions stay in the range $[0,1]$
> - The family does not oscillate wildly
>
> So the sequence is:
> - bounded
> - equicontinuous on $[0,1]$
>
> This means we can extract a **uniformly convergent subsequence**.
>
> ---
>
> ## Key idea
>
> Equicontinuity means:
> all functions in the family change at a controlled rate — no function behaves “more wildly” than the others.
>
> ---
>
> ## Why this matters
>
> This theorem is a key compactness tool:
> it tells us when a family of continuous functions behaves like a compact set under uniform convergence.
### Definition 1.4
(i) Set of continuous **Functions**, $u : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$, which have all partial derivatives, $D^a u$, $a \in \mathcal{A}_m$, $0 \leq m \leq k$, is **$C^k(\Omega)$**.

> [!definition] Multi-Index Derivative
> Let
> $$
> a=(a_1,\dots,a_n)
> $$
> be a **multi-index**, where each $a_i$ is a non-negative integer. The **order** of $a$ is
> $$
> |a| = a_1+\cdots+a_n.
> $$
>
> For a sufficiently differentiable function $u:\Omega\to\mathbb{R}$, the derivative corresponding to the multi-index $a$ is
> $$
> D^a u
> =
> \frac{\partial^{|a|}u}
> {\partial x_1^{a_1}\cdots\partial x_n^{a_n}}.
> $$
>
> In other words, $a_i$ tells us how many times to differentiate with respect to $x_i$.

> [!example] Computing $D^a u$
> Let
> $$
> u(x,y)=x^2y^3
> $$
> and choose the multi-index
> $$
> a=(1,2).
> $$
>
> Then
> $$
> D^a u
> =
> \frac{\partial^3u}{\partial x\,\partial y^2}.
> $$
>
> Differentiating:
> $$
> u_x = 2xy^3,
> $$
> $$
> u_{xy}=6xy^2,
> $$
> $$
> u_{xyy}=12xy.
> $$
>
> Hence
> $$
> D^{(1,2)}u = 12xy.
> $$

> [!example] Function in $C^2(\Omega)$
> Consider
> $$
> u(x,y)=x^2y^3.
> $$
>
> Its first derivatives are
> $$
> u_x=2xy^3,
> \qquad
> u_y=3x^2y^2,
> $$
>
> and its second derivatives are
> $$
> u_{xx}=2y^3,
> \qquad
> u_{xy}=6xy^2,
> \qquad
> u_{yy}=6x^2y.
> $$
>
> All of these derivatives are continuous functions. Therefore
> $$
> u\in C^2(\Omega).
> $$
>
> In fact, since every derivative of a polynomial is again a polynomial, we have
> $$
> u\in C^k(\Omega)
> $$
> for every $k\geq0$.

> [!tip] Intuition for $C^k(\Omega)$
> A function belongs to $C^k(\Omega)$ if:
>
> - all partial derivatives up to order $k$ exist,
> - all of those derivatives are continuous.
>
> Think of:
>
> - $C^0(\Omega)$ = continuous functions,
> - $C^1(\Omega)$ = continuous functions with continuous first derivatives,
> - $C^2(\Omega)$ = continuous functions with continuous first and second derivatives,
> - and so on.
>
> The larger $k$ is, the smoother the function is.

(ii) $C^k(\overline{\Omega})$ is the set of functions whose derivatives up to the order $k$ can be extended continuously to $\overline{\Omega}$. It is equipped with the following norm, 
$$
\|u\|_{C^k} = \max_{0\leq|a|\leq k} \sup_{x\in\overline{\Omega}}|D^a u(x)|
$$

> [!example] Computing a $C^1$ Norm
> Let
> $$
> \Omega=[0,1]
> $$
> and
> $$
> u(x)=x^2.
> $$
>
> Since $k=1$, we must consider:
> $$
> u(x)=x^2,
> \qquad
> u'(x)=2x.
> $$
>
> The largest value of $|u(x)|$ on $[0,1]$ is
> $$
> \sup_{x\in[0,1]}|u(x)|=1.
> $$
>
> The largest value of $|u'(x)|$ on $[0,1]$ is
> $$
> \sup_{x\in[0,1]}|u'(x)|=2.
> $$
>
> Therefore
> $$
> \|u\|_{C^1}
> =
> \max\{1,2\}
> =
> 2.
> $$

> [!example] Computing a $C^2$ Norm
> For the same function
> $$
> u(x)=x^2,
> $$
> we also include the second derivative:
> $$
> u''(x)=2.
> $$
>
> Thus
> $$
> \sup |u| = 1,
> \qquad
> \sup |u'| = 2,
> \qquad
> \sup |u''| = 2.
> $$
>
> Hence
> $$
> \|u\|_{C^2}
> =
> \max\{1,2,2\}
> =
> 2.
> $$

(iii) $C^k_0(\Omega) = C^k(\Omega)\cap C_0(\Omega)$

(iv) $C^\infty(\Omega) = \cap_{k=0}^{\infty} C^k(\Omega)$

(v) $C^\infty_0(\Omega) = C^\infty(\Omega) \cap C_0(\Omega)$

## Hölder continuous functions
### Definition 1.7
Let $D \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, $u:D\rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ adn $0< \alpha \leq1$. we let 
$$
[u]_{C^{0,\alpha}(D)} = \sup_{x,y\in D} \left\{ \frac{|u(x)-u(y)|}{|x-y|^\alpha} \right\}
$$
This is called **Hölder seminorm**

(i) $C^{0,\alpha}(\Omega)$ is the set of $u\in C(\Omega)$ such that 
$$
[u]_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)} < \infty \qquad \forall \text{ compact set } K \subset \Omega
$$
(ii) similar for $\overline{\Omega}$, It has norm,
$$
\|u\|_{C^{0,\alpha}} = \|u\|_{C^0} + [u]_{C^{0,\alpha}}
$$

> [!tip] Example: Hölder continuous function and computation of the norm
> Let $D = [0,1]$ and define
> $$
> u(x) = \sqrt{x}.
> $$
>
> We compute the Hölder norm in $C^{0,1/2}([0,1])$.
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 1: $C^0$ norm
> $$
> \|u\|_{C^0} = \sup_{x \in [0,1]} |\sqrt{x}| = 1.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 2: Hölder seminorm
> Take $\alpha = \tfrac12$:
> $$
> [u]_{C^{0,1/2}} = \sup_{x \ne y} \frac{|\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y}|}{|x-y|^{1/2}}.
> $$
>
> Use the identity:
> $$
> |\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y}| = \frac{|x-y|}{\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}}.
> $$
>
> So:
> $$
> \frac{|\sqrt{x}-\sqrt{y}|}{|x-y|^{1/2}}
> =
> \frac{|x-y|^{1/2}}{\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{y}}.
> $$
>
> The worst case occurs when $x,y \to 0$, giving:
> $$
> [u]_{C^{0,1/2}} = 1.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 3: Full Hölder norm
> $$
> \|u\|_{C^{0,1/2}} = \|u\|_{C^0} + [u]_{C^{0,1/2}} = 1 + 1 = 2.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Intuition
> - $u(x)=\sqrt{x}$ is continuous but not Lipschitz.
> - It *is* Hölder continuous with exponent $1/2$.
> - The Hölder seminorm measures how singular the function is near points where it becomes steep (here near $x=0$).

(iii) $C^{k,\alpha}(\Omega)$ is the set of $u\in C^k(\Omega)$ such that 
$$
[D^a u]_{C^{0,\alpha}(K)} < \infty \qquad \forall \text{ compact set } K \subset \Omega \text{ and } a \in \mathcal{A}_k
$$
(ii) similar for $\overline{\Omega}$, It has norm,
$$
\|u\|_{C^{k,\alpha}} = \|u\|_{C^k} + \max_{a \in \mathcal{A}_k}[D^a u]_{C^{0,\alpha}}
$$

> [!tip] Example: computing a $C^{1,\alpha}$ norm in $\mathbb{R}^2$
> Let $\Omega = [0,1]^2$ and define
> $$
> u(x,y)=x^2+y^2.
> $$
>
> We compute the $C^{1,\alpha}(\Omega)$ norm.
>
> We first compute partial derivatives:
> $$
> u_x = 2x, \qquad u_y = 2y.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 1: $C^1$ norm
>
> We take sup norms over $\Omega$:
> $$
> \sup_{\Omega} |u| = 2,
> \qquad
> \sup_{\Omega} |u_x| = 2,
> \qquad
> \sup_{\Omega} |u_y| = 2.
> $$
>
> So:
> $$
> \|u\|_{C^1} = 2.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 2: Hölder seminorm of first derivatives
>
> We compute:
> $$
> [u_x]_{C^{0,\alpha}} =
> \sup_{(x,y)\ne(x',y')} \frac{|2x-2x'|}{\|(x,y)-(x',y')\|^\alpha},
> $$
>
> and similarly for $u_y$:
> $$
> [u_y]_{C^{0,\alpha}} =
> \sup_{(x,y)\ne(x',y')} \frac{|2y-2y'|}{\|(x,y)-(x',y')\|^\alpha}.
> $$
>
> Since each derivative is linear in one variable, both are bounded by a constant (here 2), so:
> $$
> \max\{[u_x]_{C^{0,\alpha}},[u_y]_{C^{0,\alpha}}\} = 2.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 3: Full $C^{1,\alpha}$ norm
>
> $$
> \|u\|_{C^{1,\alpha}} = \|u\|_{C^1} + \max([u_x]_{C^{0,\alpha}},[u_y]_{C^{0,\alpha}}) = 2 + 2 = 4.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> > [!intuition]
> > Even in multiple dimensions:
> > - $C^1$ controls size of function + all first derivatives
> > - Hölder part controls how those derivatives vary in space
> > - multi-variable case just repeats the same idea in each coordinate direction

### Remark 1.8
(iii) when $\alpha = 1$, $C^{0,1}(\overline{\Omega})$ is the set of **Lipschitz continuous functions**. There exisist a constant $\gamma >0$, 
$$
|u(x) -u(y)| \leq \gamma|x-y|, \quad \forall x,y \in (\overline{\Omega})
$$
The best is $\gamma = [u]_{C^{0,1}}$

### Example 1.9
Let $\Omega = (0,1)$ and $u_\alpha(x) = x^\alpha$ with $\alpha \in [0,1]$. If $0< \alpha \leq 1$, then
$$
[u_\alpha]_{C^{0,\alpha}} = \sup_{x \ne y} \frac{|x^\alpha - y^\alpha|}{|x-y|^\alpha} = 1, \qquad x,y,\in [0,1].
$$

### Proposition 1.10 
(i) If $u,v \in C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})$ then $uv \in C^{0,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega})$

(ii) If $0\leq\alpha\leq\beta\leq1$ and $k\in\mathbb{Z}^+$ then 
$$
C^k(\overline{\Omega}) \supset C^{k,\alpha}(\overline{\Omega}) \supset C^{k,\beta}(\overline{\Omega}) \supset C^{k,1}(\overline{\Omega})
$$

(iii) If, $\Omega$ is bounded and convex, then
$$
C^{k,1}(\overline{\Omega}) \supset C^{k+1}(\overline{\Omega})
$$
