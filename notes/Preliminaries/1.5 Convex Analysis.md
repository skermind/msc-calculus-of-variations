> [!intuition] Mental picture (very useful for Direct Method)
>
> **Convex set:** you can draw a straight line between any two points and the entire line stays inside the set.  
>
> **Convex function:** the graph lies *below its chords* — i.e. any line segment between two points on the graph sits above (or on) the graph.
>
> 👉 Set = where you are allowed to move  
> 👉 Function = shape of the energy landscape
### Definition 1.49 
(i) **Convex Set** The set $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ is said to be convex if from for every $x,y \in \Omega$ and every $\lambda \in [0,1]$ we have $\lambda x + (1-\lambda)y \in \Omega$.

> [!example] Convex Set Example
>
> Let
>
> $$
> \Omega=(0,1).
> $$
>
> Choose
>
> $$
> x=0.2,\qquad y=0.8,\qquad \lambda=0.25.
> $$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> \lambda x + (1-\lambda)y
> =
> 0.25(0.2)+0.75(0.8)
> =
> 0.65.
> $$
>
> Since $0.65 \in (0,1)$, the point remains in the set.
>
> In fact, every weighted average of two points in $(0,1)$ remains in $(0,1)$.
>
> Therefore $(0,1)$ is convex.
>
> **Intuition:** Every point on the straight line segment joining two points of the set remains inside the set.

(ii) **Convex Function**  Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be convex. The Function $f : \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ is said to be convex if for every $x,y\in \Omega$ and every $\lambda \in [0,1]$, the following inequality holds

$$
f(x) \ge f(y) + \langle \nabla f(y);x-y\rangle, \forall x,y \in \mathbb{R}^n
$$

> [!example] Convex Function Example (gradient form)
>
> Let $\Omega = \mathbb{R}^n$ and define
>
> $$
> f(x) = |x|^2.
> $$
>
> Then
>
> $$
> \nabla f(x) = 2x.
> $$
>
> Take $x,y \in \mathbb{R}^n$. We check the convexity inequality:
>
> $$
> f(x) \ge f(y) + \langle \nabla f(y), x - y \rangle.
> $$
>
> Substitute:
>
> $$
> f(x) = |x|^2,\quad f(y) = |y|^2,\quad \nabla f(y)=2y.
> $$
>
> So the right-hand side becomes:
>
> $$
> f(y) + \langle \nabla f(y), x - y \rangle
> = |y|^2 + \langle 2y, x - y \rangle.
> $$
>
> Expand the scalar product:
>
> $$
> = |y|^2 + 2\langle y, x \rangle - 2|y|^2
> = 2\langle x, y \rangle - |y|^2.
> $$
>
> Now compare with the left-hand side:
>
> $$
> |x|^2 - \left(2\langle x, y \rangle - |y|^2\right)
> = |x|^2 - 2\langle x, y \rangle + |y|^2
> = |x - y|^2 \ge 0.
> $$
>
> Hence:
>
> $$
> f(x) \ge f(y) + \langle \nabla f(y), x - y \rangle.
> $$
>
> So $f(x)=|x|^2$ is convex.

>```


### Theorem 1.50
Let $f : \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ and $f \in C^1(\mathbb{R})$.
(i) The function f is convex If and only If

$$
f(x) \ge f(y) + \langle \nabla f(y);x-y\rangle, \forall x,y \in \mathbb{R}^n
$$

(ii) if $f\in C^2(\mathbb{R}^n)$, then $f$ is convex if and only if its hessian, $\nabla^2 f$ is positive semi definite. 

> [!intuition] Convexity via the Hessian
> ### What is the Hessian?
>
> The **Hessian** is the matrix of all second derivatives:
>
> $$
> \nabla^2 f(x) =
> \begin{pmatrix}
> \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1^2} & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_1 \partial x_n} \\
> \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\
> \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n \partial x_1} & \cdots & \frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial x_n^2}
> \end{pmatrix}
> $$
>
> It measures **curvature in every direction**.
>
> ---
>
> ### What does “positive semi-definite” mean?
>
> A matrix $A$ is **positive semi-definite (PSD)** if:
>
> $$
> \langle Av, v \rangle \ge 0 \quad \text{for all } v \in \mathbb{R}^n.
> $$
>
> 👉 Interpretation:
>
> The function curves **upwards in every direction** (never bends downwards).
>
> ---
>
> ### Intuition
>
> - Gradient tells you slope (first derivative)
> - Hessian tells you curvature (second derivative)
>
> Convexity means:
>
> > “No matter which direction you move, the graph never bends downward.”
>
> ---
>
> ### Example: $f(x)=|x|^2 = x^T x$
>
> Compute:
>
> $$
> \nabla f(x) = 2x
> $$
>
> $$
> \nabla^2 f(x) = 2I
> $$
>
> Now check PSD:
>
> $$
> \langle 2Iv, v \rangle = 2|v|^2 \ge 0
> $$
>
> So $2I$ is positive definite ⇒ $f$ is convex.
>
> ---
>
> ### Picture in words
>
> - Convex function = bowl shape
> - Hessian PSD = bowl curves upward in every direction
>
> 👉 “No direction of negative curvature = convex function”

### Theorem 1.51 (Jensen inequality) 
> [!intuition] Measure of a set (meas Ω)
>
> ### Definition
>
> For a set $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, the notation
>
> $$
> \mathrm{meas}(\Omega)
> $$
>
> means the **Lebesgue measure** of $\Omega$, i.e. its “size”:
>
> - length in $\mathbb{R}$
> - area in $\mathbb{R}^2$
> - volume in $\mathbb{R}^3$
>
> ---
>
> ### Examples
>
> **1D (length):**
> $$
> \Omega = (0,5)
> $$
> $$
> \mathrm{meas}(\Omega) = 5
> $$
>
> ---
>
> **2D (area):**
> $$
> \Omega = (0,2)\times(0,3)
> $$
> $$
> \mathrm{meas}(\Omega) = 2 \cdot 3 = 6
> $$
>
> ---
>
> **Unit square:**
> $$
> \Omega = (0,1)\times(0,1)
> $$
> $$
> \mathrm{meas}(\Omega) = 1
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Intuition
>
> > Measure tells you how much space the set occupies.
>
> Think:
> - interval → stretch of line  
> - rectangle → amount of area  
> - box → amount of volume
>
> It is the rigorous version of “size” used in analysis and Sobolev spaces.

Let $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^n$ be open and bounded, $u \in L^1(\Omega)$ and $f : \mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be convex, then 

$$
f\left( \frac{1}{\mathrm{meas}(\Omega)}\int_\Omega u(x)\, \mathrm{d}x\right) \le \frac{1}{\mathrm{meas}(\Omega)}\int_\Omega f(u(x))\, \mathrm{d}x
$$

> [!example] Jensen’s Inequality Example
>
> Let $\Omega = (0,1)$ so that $\mathrm{meas}(\Omega)=1$, and define
>
> $$
> u(x) = x.
> $$
>
> Take a convex function:
>
> $$
> f(t) = t^2.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 1: Average of $u$
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{\mathrm{meas}(\Omega)} \int_\Omega u(x)\,dx
> = \int_0^1 x\,dx
> = \frac{1}{2}.
> $$
>
> So:
>
> $$
> f\!\left(\int_0^1 x\,dx\right)
> = f\!\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)
> = \frac{1}{4}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 2: Average of $f(u)$
>
> $$
> \int_0^1 f(u(x))\,dx
> = \int_0^1 x^2\,dx
> = \frac{1}{3}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Step 3: Compare
>
> $$
> \frac{1}{4} \le \frac{1}{3}.
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Conclusion
>
> Jensen’s inequality holds:
>
> $$
> f\left(\int_\Omega u\right)
> \le
> \int_\Omega f(u).
> $$
>
> ---
>
> ### Intuition
>
> > “Applying a convex function after averaging gives a smaller result than averaging after applying the convex function.”
>
> Convexity pushes the graph upward, so averaging before applying $f$ loses curvature information.
