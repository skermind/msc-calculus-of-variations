\textit{The calculus of variations is one of the classical branches of
mathematics. It was Euler who, looking at the work of Lagrange, gave the
present name, not really self explanatory, to this field of mathematics.}\cite[p.~1]{dacorogna}

In this dissertation, I explore applications of the Direct Method of the Calculus of Variations. The Calculus of Variations is concerned with optimisation problems where the quantity minimised or maximised depends upon a function. Although many variational problems can be stated in relatively simple terms, their solutions are often much more complex. Establishing the existence of an optimal function is not always straightforward. This can lead to non-linear differential equations that are difficult to solve. 

The Classical Method of the Calculus of Variations is one approach to such problems. The conditions for a minimiser can be derived from the Euler--Lagrange equation. For fairly simple problems, this equation can be solved directly. However, as the functional becomes more complicated, the Euler--Lagrange equation may become a non-linear boundary-value problem that is dificult or impossible to obtain. This motivates the study of alternative approaches.

The main focus of this dissertation is the Direct Method of the Calculus of Variations. Rather than attempting to explicitly solve the Euler--Lagrange equation, the Direct Method seeks to establish the existence of a minimiser directly through the properties of the admissible space and the functional being minimised. The method relies upon ideas from functional analysis, including weak convergence, weak compactness, coercivity, and weak lower semicontinuity. A substantial part of the dissertation is devoted to developing the functional analysis background required to understand these ideas. We will be following Dacorogna's \textit{Introduction to the Calculus of Variations} \cite{dacorogna}.

The Classical Method is introduced through a number of simpler variational problems. Building upon the necessary results from functional analysis, the Direct Method is then developed and applied to establish the existence of minimisers for suitable variational problems.

The ideas developed throughout the dissertation are then applied to a path-planning problem. We want to plan routes on a 2D plane with two boundary points. We will be wanting to minimise the cost of the path along siad plane, which is made up of the terrain cost, a curvature penalty and a length penalty. The resulting problem therefore seeks a route that which will travel through lower-cost regions, avoid unnecessary detours, and maintain a degree of geometric smoothness.

The inclusion of this curvature term leads to a second-order variational problem and introduces analytical complexity. Although the Direct Method can be used to investigate the existence of minimisers, we do not get an explicit representation of the path. The corresponding Euler--Lagrange equation for this model leads to a non-linear fourth-order problem. For this reason, the final part of the dissertation considers the numerical approximation of optimal paths.

A stochastic optimisation method based on Simulated Annealing is developed to investigate this path-planning problem computationally. The continuous problem is discretised, allowing candidate paths to be represented numerically and iteratively modified in search of lower cost paths. The numerical experiments complement the theoretical analysis by searching for approximate solutions and illustrating the effect of the different terms within the functional.

The dissertation aims to connect the Direct Method with a optimisation problem and its numerical approximation. The progression from functional analysis, through the Classical and Direct Methods of the Calculus of Variations, to a numerical investigation of a path-planning problem reflects a central objective of this dissertation. 

\section{Background}
\label{sec:backgound}

The problems typically associated with the calculus of variations are ancient in comparison with many of the problems considered in modern mathematics, although their formulations can be   simple to understand. At its core, the calculus of variations concerns optimisation problems in which one seeks to determine an optimal function with respect to an objective functional. Simple examples include finding the shortest path between two points in a plane or determining the maximum area enclosed by a fixed length of fencing.

Although the formulation of such problems may be relatively straightforward, the methods required to obtain their solutions can be considerably more involved. The development of a systematic framework for the calculus of variations therefore allows more abstract and challenging problems to be considered. One such example is the problem of determining the optimal course of a sailing boat travelling against the wind, as discussed in Rindler’s lecture notes, \textit{Introduction to the Modern Calculus of Variations}.\cite{rindler}

As in many works on the Calculus of Variations, I introduce the subject through Dido’s problem. The semi-historical Phoenician princess, and later and most famously the founder and queen of Carthage, is said to have encountered a variational problem within the founding myth of her city. According to the legend, Dido was offered as much land as could be enclosed by the hide of a bull. She subsequently cut the hide into thin strips and used them to enclose a large area of land, which became the site of Carthage. In doing so, Dido is said to have encountered what is now known as the \textit{isoperimetric problem}: \textit{Find, among all curves of given length, the one which encloses the maximal area} \cite{bandle}.

It was this problem that inspired Euler to seek a general method for solving problems in the Calculus of Variations, laying some of the foundations of the subject. Eleven years after Euler’s work, the nineteen-year-old Lagrange developed a more general method, which impressed Euler \cite{bandle}. The Classical Method of the Calculus of Variations is therefore closely associated with the work of these two mathematical giants. 

Following the development of the Classical Method, what is now known as the Direct Method was conceived through the work of mathematicians including Hilbert, Lebesgue, and Tonelli. Among the problems that motivated this transition was the Dirichlet integral: a problem of multiple integrals \cite{dacorogna}. Hilbert played an important role in the transition of the Calculus of Variations into the modern era, particularly through the 19th, 20th, and 23rd problems that he presented at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Paris in 1900 \cite{rindler}.

This brings us to the present day, where the field of the Calculus of Variations is both broad and active. Mathematicians continue to develop the theory and apply it to an increasingly diverse range of problems, leaving their mark on the subject across areas of both pure and applied mathematics.

\section{Problem Choice}

It was initially hard for me to come to a decision regarding which problem to work on. The Calculus of Variations as a mathematical discipline has applications in a vast number of fields. After reading about Dido's problem and having a first discussion with my supervisor about possible problems, I was particularly attracted to path-planning problems.

At the start of my dissertation, I had begun reading about applications of optimisation and variational methods in economics and finance, including the work of Kamien and Schwartz \cite{kamien1981dynamic} and Malliavin and Thalmaier \cite{malliavin2006stochastic}. This reading made clear that I lacked some of the analytical foundations required to pursue these problems rigorously. In particular, I understood the importance of developing a stronger functional-analytis foundations underlying the Direct Method of the Calculus of Variations. Following discussions with my supervisor, it was therefore decided that the dissertation would focus on developing these foundations, using Dacorogna's \textit{Introduction to the Calculus of Variations} \cite{dacorogna} as a key reference.

We considered a modified version of Dido's problem in which the objective is no longer to maximise area with a length constraint, but to determine an efficient path through a spatially varying cost field. The model includes penalties for  both the length and curvature of the path. This formulation was motivated in part by a video from Wendover Productions \cite{wendover2026} discussing the possible routes for the California High-Speed Rail project. The selection of a high-speed rail route must consider the different costs for each path, while also keeping journey length short and the geometric restrictions of high-speed rail.

The spatially varying cost field represents the differing costs of construction across a region, the length term penalises unnecessarily long routes, and the curvature term accounts for the geometric requirements of the railway. Although the motivating application is railway planning, the same mathematics could be applied to other large-scale infrastructure problems, such as motorway or pipeline route planning.

This problem was ultimately chosen because it provides both a meaningful application and a natural setting in which to investigate the Direct Method.
